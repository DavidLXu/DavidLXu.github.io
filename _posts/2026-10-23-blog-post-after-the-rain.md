# **AFTER THE RAIN**

### **A Record of Small Occurrences**

The dripping eaves had fallen silent; the courtyard pools had yet to dry. In a small chamber to the west, the lattice window stood half-shut. Dust gathered over the years, dampened by the lingering rains, clung darkly to decaying paper and tattered silk. Moss mottled the steps; weeds spread through the joints of the paving. Several tall bamboos, broken by last night’s gale, lay crooked and prostrate beside the path to the rear garden. One stem still bore withered sheaths at its tip; struck now and then by drops from the eaves, it trembled faintly, as though something concealed beneath it stirred, then held still.

At dawn I opened the door. The covered passage was damp, its brickwork veined with a fine, salt-white bloom. Taking up a worn broom, I set out to clear the litter from the steps. Before reaching the courtyard’s centre, I heard a rustling from the eastern chamber. At first I suspected mice gnawing at wicker trunks; yet the sound came at measured intervals, like a fingernail lightly scraping bamboo strips, or thin porcelain touching porcelain. Following it, I found an old wicker chest leaning against the wall, its lid ajar, a corner of russet cloth showing through the gap, darkened by long damp. As I bent to move it, I discovered a cat curled beneath: brindled, half its left ear missing. It did not flee, but watched me sidelong through narrowed eyes, a low murmur rising in its throat.

Supposing it hungry, I went to the kitchen for food. The hearth held undisturbed cold ashes; grime coated the pot; yesterday’s gruel had soured, and the grain jar was empty. Searching the cupboards, I found a small dish of leftover dried fish, still pungent but too heavily salted to offer it. After some hesitation, I remembered half a steamed cake upon my table. I broke off a piece, moistened it with warm water, and laid it upon a brick. The cat approached and sniffed, then drew back. Thrice it circled the dish, yet would not eat.

The daylight grew clearer. Through a rent in the clouds fell a shaft of pale amber, illuminating cobwebs hanging from the eaves, their clustered dewdrops shimmering and swaying. Beyond the wall an old woman called to her grandson, her voice borne brokenly upon the damp wind; farther away a well-wheel creaked, as though someone were drawing water. I was about to return indoors when the cat seized the cake and hurried towards the rear garden, passing through the broken fence. Surprised, I followed quietly.

The rear garden had long lain neglected. Mugwort rose to the knee; thorns caught at my clothes. The hibiscus planted in former years stood half withered, though several fresh shoots had sprung from its roots. In a corner by the wall lay an overturned earthen jar, its mouth against the ground, with decaying leaves and branches heaped beside it. The cat slipped into a narrow space beside the jar. Presently a faint mewling arose. Parting the dead leaves, I found three kittens huddled together, their eyes still closed, their downy fur damp, their small bodies pressing against one another. Their mother returned with the cake, laid it nearby, then settled beside them and licked each in turn.

I understood at last. Yet their shelter lay low and damp, rainwater still seeping through the earth; fearing the return of the night’s chill, I went back to the chamber, fetched an old box, lined it with worn cotton, and placed it beneath the sheltered passage. Returning to the garden, I approached to carry the kittens away. At once the cat arched its back, its fur bristling, and hissed through bared teeth, utterly unlike the creature I had seen before. I stopped and withdrew. After waiting some time, I set the box nearby and retreated farther.

Noon had passed, and the courtyard pools were slowly drying. When I returned to the garden, all was silent beside the broken jar. Only disturbed leaves revealed a few fresh pawprints beneath. Turning towards the passage, I saw soft fur stirring within the old box. The mother lay stretched outside it, eyes half-closed, the tip of her tail stirring now and then. The cake I had placed upon the brick was nowhere to be seen.

I disturbed them no further. Closing the door, I returned to my room, only then noticing the mud upon my sleeves and the moss beneath my shoes. The steps I had meant to sweep that morning remained as littered as before.

余晨起启扉，见廊庑阴湿，砖隙间浮出细白盐霜似的痕迹，遂取敝帚，欲稍芟除阶前狼藉。未及庭心，忽闻东厢传来窸窣之声，初疑鼠啮箧笥，继而觉其声间歇有致，若指甲轻搔竹篾，又若薄瓷相磕。循声而往，见一旧藤笥斜倚壁隅，笥盖微启，缝中露出一角赭色织物，潮气浸淫，颜色已黯。俯身挪之，底下竟蜷伏一狸奴，毛色狸杂，左耳缺其半，见人不遁，惟眯目睥睨，喉间作低微呜咽。

余以为饥，乃往灶间觅食。灶膛冷灰未拨，釜底凝垢，昨夕所遗糜粥已馊，瓮中亦无余粮。遍检橱柜，得一小碟残鲞，腥膻犹存，然盐渍过重，不宜饲之。踟蹰少顷，忽忆案头尚有半块蒸饼，遂折其一隅，蘸以温水，置于瓦甓之上。狸奴趋前嗅之，旋即却步，复绕碟三匝，竟不肯食。

时天光渐朗，云罅漏出一线淡赭，映照檐角悬垂的蛛丝，丝上露珠累累，光影摇曳。邻舍老妇隔墙呼其孙，声音为湿风所挟，断续难辨；远处辘轳轧轧，似有人汲井。余方欲返室，忽见狸奴衔起蒸饼，疾趋后圃，越断篱而去。余讶其举，因蹑足随之。

后圃荒秽久矣。蒿艾没膝，荆棘钩衣，旧日栽植的木槿已半枯，惟根际萌出数茎新蘖。墙根一隅，覆有破瓮，瓮口向地，旁堆败叶腐柯。狸奴至此，俯首钻入瓮旁隙地，俄而闻细细啾唧之声。余拨开枯叶，见三只乳猫偎作一团，眼尚未启，身上绒毛沾湿，正相互拱挤。其母衔饼而归，置于近旁，旋卧其侧，以舌遍舐诸雏。

余乃恍然。然见其栖处卑湿，雨后积水犹渗，恐夜间寒露复侵，遂返阁中，取废箧一具，垫以敝絮，移置廊下避风处。复往圃中，欲携诸雏归。狸奴见余近前，倏然弓背，鬣毛尽竖，齿间发嘶嘶声，较先前判若两物。余止步，退而候之，良久乃以旧箧置其近侧，自行远避。

日已过午，庭中积潦渐涸。余复往视，破瓮旁寂然无声，唯败叶翻卷，露出底下几道新鲜爪痕。转至廊下，则见旧箧中绒毛攒动，狸奴横卧其外，半阖双目，尾尖偶尔轻轻一摆。先前置于瓦甓上的蒸饼，亦已不知所终。

余未复扰之。掩扉归室，始觉衣袂沾泥，履底黏苔，而晨起欲扫之阶除，狼藉依旧。
